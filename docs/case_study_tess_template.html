TESS: a stable lunar-resonant orbit, recovered from sky-survey sightings

NASA's planet-hunting telescope TESS flies a high, elongated orbit locked in step with the Moon: on average, two orbits for each lunar month. Asteroid surveys keep spotting it as an unidentified moving object. This case study fits an orbit to 20 of those survey sightings from late 2020 with the cislunar toolkit. NASA's own tracked trajectory then grades each step: the fit, the predictions, and the stability analysis.

Headline results
How this differs from the Chang'e case study The Chang'e 5-T1 case study had five days of dense tracking of a dead rocket stage on a doomed orbit, and only one point of truth: the crater. Here the data are sparse, three short visits spread over two months, but the truth is known at every instant. The orbit is also built to survive, not to crash.

1. The spacecraft and its orbit

TESS (Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite, launched 18 April 2018) needed a stable orbit that stays far from Earth for most of the time, with a close pass every couple of weeks to send data home. It uses a P/2 lunar-resonant orbit: an average period of half a lunar month, about 13.7 days, with perigee near 100,000 km and apogee near 400,000 km. The orbit is phased so that TESS is at apogee when the Moon is about 90° away. The Moon's pulls then roughly cancel over each lunar month instead of building up. NASA expected the orbit to stay stable for decades without station-keeping. The Sun and Moon make the instantaneous period swing around the average: in late 2020 it was about 14.1–14.4 days.

In the language of this toolkit, TESS flies close to a 2:1 resonant periodic orbit of the Earth–Moon three-body problem, one of the "rosettes" in the user manual. Section 8 puts the two side by side.

Sources: Project Pluto pseudo-MPEC for 2018-038A (astrometry and Find_Orb orbit); NASA's trajectory from JPL Horizons (target −95); observatory constants from MPC.

2. The sightings

The 134 sightings come mostly from the Catalina Sky Survey and the Mt. Lemmon Survey in Arizona. These are asteroid hunters that photograph the sky every clear night. TESS shows up in their images as a magnitude-17 moving dot and gets reported as a possible near-Earth asteroid, which explains labels like ZR38AE8 in the data file. The sightings are bursts of 3–4 frames, a few nights a month at best.

4. One night is not enough

Suppose only the last night, 20 November 2020, were available:

A single night gives position and direction of motion well, but distance and speed poorly. The orbital period is then uncertain by hours. After four orbits (two months back to September) the predicted position can be tens of thousands of kilometres off, much too far to say which faint dot in an old image was TESS. Section 6 shows this curve.

5. Three nights, two months

Adding the sightings from 24 September and 8 October links three visits four orbits apart. The orbital period is then pinned down to seconds. Over two months sunlight pressure also becomes measurable. TESS's solar panels and sunshade are large for its mass, so sunlight pushes noticeably on the orbit. The fit was repeated for a range of sunlight-pressure values (CrA/m, reflectivity × area ÷ mass):

7. Is TESS just coasting?

A natural object would coast, so one unpowered trajectory could match its whole path. A spacecraft occasionally fires thrusters: TESS uses them to unload momentum from its reaction wheels. To check, an unpowered trajectory was fitted to each 10-day stretch of NASA's own trajectory:

Figure 5. How well one unpowered arc matches 10 days of NASA's trajectory

8. Why the orbit is stable

The three-body picture. In a frame that rotates with the Moon, a 2:1 resonant orbit traces a closed two-lobed curve. The toolkit computes it as a periodic orbit of the Earth–Moon CR3BP (periodic.resonant(2, 1, e)). Its stability index is , where 1 means linearly stable: small nudges stay small instead of growing. TESS's real path, from NASA's trajectory, follows the same pattern:

Figure 6. TESS in the Earth–Moon rotating frame
Earth at the origin, Moon fixed on the right (the frame turns with the Moon and is rescaled to its mean distance). Grey: the CR3BP 2:1 resonant periodic orbit with TESS's eccentricity. Blue: TESS from 24 September to 18 November 2020. The real orbit is tilted and perturbed by the Sun, so it wanders around the ideal curve, but it never strays far.

Ten years ahead. The fitted state was propagated for ten years with Earth, Moon, Sun and sunlight pressure, together with NASA's own state and twelve samples drawn from the fit's uncertainty:

Figure 7. Perigee and apogee for ten years
One dot per orbit. Blue is the fitted orbit, grey the twelve uncertainty samples, and orange circles are NASA's 20 November 2020 state run through the same model. They lie almost exactly on top of each other: the orbit's slow ten-year breathing doesn't depend on the details of the starting state. No thruster firings are included.
Figure 8. The resonance lock: angle between TESS and the Moon at each apogee
For a P/2 orbit phased like TESS's this stays near 90°, so the Moon never lines up with apogee and the perturbations average out. If the lock broke, this angle would sweep through 0°–180°.

10. Limits

  • The fit assumes TESS coasts between 24 September and 20 November. Section 7 suggests NASA's trajectory contains small events in that span. They cost the fit some accuracy, and the reported uncertainty does not include them.
  • Sunlight pressure is modelled as a uniform push away from the Sun (a "cannonball"). TESS keeps its sunshade toward the Sun, so this is a reasonable first approximation. Earth-shadow passages are not modelled.
  • The ten-year runs show the natural, unpowered behaviour. Real operations add small thruster firings, and NASA has adjusted TESS's orbit.
  • The CR3BP picture is planar and circular. It explains why the orbit is stable but not every wiggle of the real path.
