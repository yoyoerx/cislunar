cislunar: a field manual for orbits between the Earth and the Moon

This manual teaches you to use the cislunar Python toolkit to design orbits around the Moon, plot them in 2D and 3D, plan transfers between them and plan rendezvous and docking. If you learned orbits by flying rockets in Kerbal Space Program, you already have good instincts. This manual shows where those instincts still work and where the real Earth–Moon system behaves differently.

Every chart in this manual was generated by the toolkit itself. The interactive panels run a small copy of the toolkit's three-body integrator in your browser, so you can make your own burns and watch the result. Nothing needs to be installed to read and play; you only need Python when you want to use the tools yourself (section 12).

How to read this manual Sections 2–5 are the concepts that make the rest make sense. Sections 6–9 are the toolkit's main capabilities, each with an interactive figure and the Python call that produced it. Sections 10–11 are advanced topics: rosette orbits, then transient orbits, flybys and Δv budgets. Sections 12–18 are reference material: installation, recipes, troubleshooting and the full API.

2. From Kerbin to cislunar space

In KSP, your ship only ever feels the gravity of one body at a time. Inside the Mun's sphere of influence (SOI) only the Mun pulls on you; outside it, only Kerbin does. Each piece of the flight is a perfect ellipse (or hyperbola), and the pieces are stitched together at the SOI boundary. Real mission designers call this the patched-conic approximation. It is a good model for interplanetary flight and a decent first guess for going to the Moon.

In reality, both the Earth and the Moon pull on your spacecraft at all times. When you are far from the Moon, Earth dominates and patched conics work well. But many of the most useful orbits for lunar missions, including NASA's Gateway orbit, spend their time around 70,000 km from the Moon. That is right at the edge of the Moon's sphere of influence (about 66,000 km), where neither body dominates. Here patched conics do not just lose accuracy; they miss whole classes of orbits that only exist because two bodies pull at once.

Cislunar ("this side of the Moon") space is the region from Earth out to just beyond the Moon. The model that captures it is the three-body problem: Earth, Moon and your spacecraft. The toolkit uses the circular restricted three-body problem (CR3BP): the Earth and Moon move in circles around their common centre of mass, and the spacecraft is too light to disturb them.

3. The rotating frame: a camera that follows the Moon

KSP's map view is usually inertial: the camera does not spin, and the Mun moves around Kerbin. Three-body work is almost always done in a rotating frame (also called the synodic frame), a camera that turns with the Moon. In it:

  • The Earth and Moon sit still on the x-axis. The origin is their centre of mass (the barycentre), about 4,670 km from the Earth's centre, still inside the Earth.
  • +x points from the Earth toward the Moon, +z points "up" out of the Moon's orbital plane, and +y is the direction the Moon is moving.
  • The frame turns once per sidereal month (about 27.3 days).

Orbits that look like messy spirals in the inertial view become closed loops in the rotating view. That is why the toolkit computes everything in the rotating frame and only converts to other views for plotting. frames.trajectory_to_inertial() does the conversion.

Figure 2. One orbit, two cameras
Left: the rotating frame, centred on the Moon. Right: an inertial frame (one that does not rotate). Press play to watch the spacecraft move in both views at once.
Watch out The toolkit's "inertial" frame lines up with the rotating frame at time zero, and its z-axis is the Moon's orbit normal. It is not the J2000 / ICRF frame used by NASA ephemerides. Rotate your state vectors into the Earth–Moon plane before handing them to the toolkit (see recipes).

4. Units you will see

Inside the toolkit, all numbers are nondimensional, meaning they are scaled so the important quantities equal 1. This keeps the maths well behaved and lets the same code work for any pair of bodies. You will see these numbers when you print raw states, so it helps to recognise them:

UnitEqualsMeaning
1 length unit (LU)Earth–Moon distance
1 time unit (TU)1/(2π) of a sidereal month: the rotating frame turns 1 radian per TU
1 velocity unit (VU)LU / TU, roughly the Moon's orbital speed
μ (mass parameter)Moon mass / (Earth + Moon mass)

The System object (cl.EARTH_MOON) converts for you: S.km(), S.mps(), S.days() go to physical units and S.nd_length(), S.nd_time_days(), S.nd_velocity() come back.

5. Lagrange points and the energy map

In the rotating frame there are five places where the Earth's pull, the Moon's pull and the rotation of the frame balance exactly. A spacecraft parked there, with zero velocity, stays put. These are the Lagrange points (also called libration points), L1 to L5. KSP's patched conics have no Lagrange points, which is why they may be new to you.

  • L1: between Earth and Moon, about 58,000 km from the Moon.
  • L2: behind the Moon, about 64,500 km beyond it.
  • L3: on the far side of the Earth.
  • L4, L5: 60° ahead of and behind the Moon along its orbit.

L1, L2 and L3 are unstable, like a ball balanced on a hilltop, but orbits around L1 and L2 are the backbone of cislunar mission design (section 6).

The Jacobi constant: energy in the rotating frame

In KSP, a coasting ship's orbital energy is fixed. In the rotating frame, the equivalent conserved quantity is the Jacobi constant C. Higher C means less energy (the sign is flipped by convention). For a given C, some regions of space are simply unreachable: you would need negative kinetic energy to be there. Their boundary is called the zero-velocity curve.

As energy rises (lower C), "gates" open at the Lagrange points: first L1 (the door between Earth and Moon), then L2 (the door out the back of the Moon), then L3, and finally L4/L5, after which nothing is forbidden. Drag the slider to watch the gates open.

6. The orbit zoo

Most useful cislunar orbits are periodic orbits: in the rotating frame they repeat the same closed loop forever (in this idealised model). They come in families: continuous sequences of orbits that change gradually, like a series of ellipses of growing size. The main families:

FamilyWhat it looks likeWhy missions use itToolkit
Lyapunovflat loop around L1 or L2 in the Moon's orbital planesimplest libration orbit; stepping stone to the otherslyapunov(1, ax_km)
Halo3D loop around L1 or L2, tilted like a halo above or below the Moonalways visible from Earth (never behind the Moon); used for relay satelliteshalo(2, az_km, "south")
NRHO (near-rectilinear halo orbit)very stretched halo: dives to a few thousand km over one lunar pole, swings out to ~70,000 km over the otherGateway's orbit: cheap to reach and leave, easy to keep stable, continuous Earth contactnrho((9, 2))
DRO (distant retrograde orbit)large flat loop around the Moon, going backwards (retrograde) in the rotating frameextremely stable: a spacecraft can sit there for years with no correction burns (Artemis I used one)dro(x_offset_km)

Tip: set Colour path by to a speed option to see the orbit as a heat map of its speed. On an NRHO the spacecraft creeps along at under 100 m/s at apolune and races past the Moon's pole at about 1.7 km/s: "rectilinear" orbits spend most of their time far away and slow, like a ball thrown straight up.

Reading the numbers

  • Perilune / apolune: closest and farthest distance from the Moon's centre. These are KSP's periapsis and apoapsis, measured from the body's centre, not its surface (subtract 1,737 km for altitude above the Moon).
  • Period: time for one loop in the rotating frame.
  • Stability index ν: how fast small errors grow. If ν = 1 the orbit is stable: errors do not grow. If ν > 1, each revolution multiplies an unlucky error by roughly 2ν. Small L1 halos have ν around 1,000, so a 1 m position error becomes 2 km after one lap. NRHOs are much gentler (ν ≈ 1.3). This is why some orbits need regular station-keeping burns.
  • Resonance: the 9:2 NRHO completes 9 laps every 2 synodic months (the 29.5-day cycle of lunar phases). This keeps it in step with the Sun and lets it avoid long eclipses.

How the toolkit finds them

You cannot just write down a periodic orbit; you have to search for it. The toolkit starts from an approximate guess (for halos, a classic formula by Richardson) and then uses differential correction: it flies the guess half an orbit, measures how far it misses a perfect mirror-image crossing of the x–z plane, and adjusts the starting state. A few repetitions converge it to about 10−11 (a few millimetres). To walk along a family it uses continuation: take a converged orbit, nudge it, re-converge, repeat. continue_arclength can even follow a family around a "fold", where the family turns back on itself. That is how the L2 halos turn into the NRHOs in Figure 4.

Python that produces the L2 family:

import cislunar as cl
from cislunar import periodic as P

start = P.halo(2, 5000.0, "south")                 # small L2 southern halo
fam = P.continue_arclength(start, 0.01,            # step size (nondimensional)
                           stop=lambda o: o.period_days < 6.0,
                           direction=(0.0, 1.0, 0.0))  # which way to walk in (x0, z0, vy0)
for o in fam[::8]:
    print(o.summary())

7. Maneuver sandbox

This is KSP's maneuver node, three-body edition. Pick a starting orbit and a point along it, then add a burn in the familiar directions:

  • Prograde / retrograde: along / against your velocity relative to the chosen reference body.
  • Normal / anti-normal: perpendicular to your orbital plane.
  • Radial out / in: within the orbital plane, away from / toward the body.

The trajectory is integrated in your browser with the same equations the toolkit uses (cr3bp.eom; a Dormand–Prince 5(4) integrator with tolerance 10−10). Things to try:

  • NRHO, 0 m/s, 120 days: the orbit repeats, but leftover numerical error slowly grows. NRHOs are only mildly unstable, so this takes months to show.
  • L1 halo, 1 m/s prograde: watch how quickly a strongly unstable orbit throws you out.
  • DRO, 20 m/s any direction: the orbit wobbles but stays around the Moon. That is stability.
  • Low lunar orbit, 820 m/s prograde: escape the Moon, and see that the Earth then takes over.
  • LEO, 3,100 m/s prograde: a trans-lunar-sized burn fired at an arbitrary moment. Apogee reaches the Moon's distance, but the Moon is not there. Timing the burn is the job of optimize_transfer (section 8).

import numpy as np, cislunar as cl
from cislunar import frames
S = cl.EARTH_MOON
nrho = cl.nrho()
x = nrho.state_at(0.25 * nrho.period)           # a quarter of the way around
v_rel = x[3:] + np.cross([0, 0, 1], x[:3] - S.pos2)   # velocity relative to the Moon, non-rotating
prograde = v_rel / np.linalg.norm(v_rel)
x[3:] += S.nd_velocity(5.0 / 1000) * prograde    # +5 m/s prograde
traj = cl.propagate(x, S.nd_time_days(30), S, n_points=3000)

8. Getting there: transfers and intercepts

Lambert's problem: the question behind every maneuver node

When you drag a maneuver node in KSP until you get an encounter, you are solving Lambert's problem by hand: "I am here now. I want to be there at that time. What velocity do I need?" With one gravitating body this has a neat solution (lambert.lambert()). With two bodies there is no formula, so the toolkit uses shooting: guess a departure velocity (using the one-body Lambert answer as the first guess), fly it, measure the miss, correct, repeat. This is targeting.shoot(), usually reached through intercept().

A rendezvous needs two burns: Δv1 to leave your orbit and Δv2 to match the target's velocity on arrival. An intercept only needs the first burn.

Porkchop plots: KSP's transfer window planner

The cost of a transfer depends on when you leave and how long you take. A porkchop plot maps total Δv over a grid of departure times and flight times, just like the transfer window planner mods for KSP. porkchop() computes one by solving a full rendezvous at every grid point.

Figure 6. DRO to NRHO porkchop: click any cell
From a 70,000 km DRO to the 9:2 NRHO, 264 rendezvous solutions (computed with refine=3). Lighter means cheaper. Click a cell to fly that transfer in the 3D view (your browser re-integrates the arc from the toolkit's departure state). The diagonal dark ridges are arrivals while the NRHO is racing through perilune, which makes velocity matching expensive.
dro, nrho = cl.dro(70000.0), cl.nrho()
t_dep = np.linspace(0, S.nd_time_days(14), 22)
tofs  = np.linspace(S.nd_time_days(2), S.nd_time_days(9), 12)
pc = cl.porkchop(dro, nrho, t_dep, tofs,        # ~1 minute for the basic sweep
                 keep_transfers=True, refine=3) # + a few minutes of clean-up passes
print(pc.best.summary())                        # cheapest transfer found
viz.plot_porkchop(pc)
About local solutions Each grid point is solved starting from its neighbour's answer. This is fast, but a cell can lock onto a more expensive solution branch. Without clean-up, this grid had a solid dark stripe at departure days 4.7–5.3 that was purely a solver artifact. refine= re-solves every cell from each neighbour's solution and keeps the cheapest, which removed the stripe and lowered the best transfer from 515 to 498 m/s. If you still see a dark island among cheap cells, re-solve it with intercept(..., seeds="all").

From low Earth orbit to the Gateway orbit

Leaving Earth works like a KSP Mun transfer: a trans-lunar injection (TLI) burn of about 3.1 km/s from low orbit, a coast of 4–5 days, then a NRHO insertion burn at arrival. The toolkit helps with two real-world details KSP hides:

  • Plane alignment. A 28.5° inclined parking orbit (Cape Canaveral's latitude) must be oriented so its plane contains the arrival point; otherwise a large plane change is needed. targeting.raan_for_target() picks the orientation (the RAAN, right ascension of the ascending node, which sets where the orbit crosses the equator).
  • Timing. optimize_transfer() first scans departure time and flight time with cheap one-body Lambert solutions, then refines the best candidates with the real three-body model.
Figure 7. LEO to NRHO, and how sensitive the TLI burn is
The toolkit's optimised transfer. Use the slider to make the TLI burn slightly too weak or too strong and watch the arrival miss grow. This is why real missions plan trajectory correction maneuvers along the way.
from cislunar import targeting, frames
from cislunar.constants import R_EARTH
nrho = cl.nrho()
inc = np.radians(28.5)
r_arr = frames.rotating_to_inertial(S.nd_time_days(4.5), nrho.state_at(S.nd_time_days(4.5)), S, "earth")[:3]
raan = targeting.raan_for_target(r_arr, inc)[0]
leo = targeting.keplerian_state_fn(R_EARTH + 400, 0.0, inc, raan, 0.0, 0.0, S)
tr = cl.optimize_transfer(leo, nrho, (0, S.nd_time_seconds(5550)),
                          (S.nd_time_days(3.5), S.nd_time_days(5.5)))   # ~2-3 minutes
print(tr.summary())

9. Rendezvous, proximity operations and docking (RPOD)

RPOD is the last few tens of kilometres: getting from "nearby" to "docked". In KSP you switch the navball to target mode and fly by relative velocity. Real operations do the same thing in a carefully defined moving frame attached to the target, the LVLH frame (local vertical, local horizontal):

AxisNamePointsKSP analogue
xV-barthe target's direction of travelthe target's prograde
y−H-baropposite the target's orbit normalthe target's anti-normal
zR-bar"down", toward the body being orbited (the Moon by default)the target's radial-in

"Approaching along V-bar from behind" means sitting on the target's track and closing in from the −x side. R-bar is drawn pointing down in all toolkit plots, as in NASA practice.

Why you cannot just point at the target and thrust

Every KSP player has tried thrusting straight at a target in orbit and watched the ship sail past above it. The Clohessy–Wiltshire (CW) equations describe this counter-intuitive relative motion for a target in a circular orbit. A burn toward the target along V-bar raises your orbit, which slows you down, so you drift backwards. Try it:

Figure 8. Relative-motion playground (target in a 400 km circular Earth orbit)
The chaser starts at rest relative to the target, then makes one burn. Analytic CW solution (relative.cw_stm). Dots mark each completed orbit (about 92 minutes).
Presets:
The RPOD cheat sheet +V-bar burn → you rise and fall behind. −V-bar burn → you sink and move ahead. Radial burn → a closed loop that returns you to your starting point one orbit later. To catch up with a target ahead of you, you burn retrograde.

CW assumes a circular orbit around one body. An NRHO is neither circular nor around one body, so for real cislunar RPOD the toolkit propagates both spacecraft in the full three-body model and expresses the result in the target's LVLH frame. linear_relative_targeting uses the target's own state transition matrix (a table of how small offsets grow over time) as a fast first guess. plan_waypoints then solves each leg exactly.

Planning an approach

An approach is a list of waypoints: where (in LVLH) the chaser should be, and when. plan_waypoints places a burn at the start of each leg so the chaser arrives at the next waypoint, plus a final burn that sets the docking speed (here 3 cm/s). Safety checks:

  • Keep-out sphere (KOS): a 200 m bubble around the target that only the final approach may enter (keep_out_violations).
  • Approach corridor: inside the KOS, stay within a cone around the docking axis (corridor_violations).
  • Passive abort safety: if a burn fails, where does the chaser drift? A safe plan never coasts into the target (passive_abort).
Figure 9. A 21-hour approach to a spacecraft at NRHO apolune
The chaser starts 20 km behind and 1 km below the target, and arrives through hold points at 5 km, 1 km, 200 m and 30 m. Tick a box to see where it would drift for 24 hours if the next burn were missed. Several of those drifts pass dangerously close: this straight-in approach is not passively safe, which passive_abort reveals.

    R.Waypoint(8,  (-5.0, 0, 0), "5 km"),
    R.Waypoint(14, (-1.0, 0, 0), "1 km"),
    R.Waypoint(18, (-0.2, 0, 0), "200 m"),
    R.Waypoint(20, (-0.03, 0, 0), "30 m"),
    R.Waypoint(21, (0.0, 0, 0), "dock"),
], S, final_rhodot_mps=(0.03, 0, 0))
print(plan.summary())
for a in R.passive_abort(plan, duration_hours=24):
    print(a.burn_index, a.min_range_km * 1000, "m")

10. Advanced: rosette orbits

Some orbits draw a flower: loop after loop, each one turned a little from the last, until the petals close up into a finished pattern. These are rosette orbits. The key idea of this section is that a rosette is a simple orbit seen from a frame that turns at a different rate. The shape you see depends as much on the camera as on the orbit.

You have seen this before Think of a Spirograph, or of a satellite's ground track. A satellite in a plain circular orbit draws a wandering, repeating pattern on the map because the planet turns underneath it. If the orbit period and the planet's day are in a whole-number ratio, the track repeats exactly: a rosette drawn on the ground. Cislunar rosettes are the same thing, with the Moon's monthly turn playing the role of the planet's daily spin.

Two kinds matter in cislunar space, and they are mirror images of each other:

Inertial view (fixed to the stars)Rotating view (fixed to the Moon)
Earth–Moon resonant orbits (10.1)almost a plain ellipse around the Earthrosette
Lunar libration orbits: NRHO, DRO, halo (10.2)rosette around the Moona single closed loop

Not to be confused with "rosette constellations" (Ballard/Walker), a way of arranging many satellites in circular orbits. That is a different use of the same word.

10.1 Earth–Moon resonant orbits

A p:q resonant orbit goes around the Earth p times while the Moon goes around q times. Because the spacecraft and the Moon then return to the same relative positions, the orbit repeats exactly in the rotating frame after about q sidereal months. Interior resonances (p > q) stay inside the Moon's orbit; exterior ones (p < q) go outside it. Rules of thumb you can check in Figure 10:

  • An interior p:q orbit has p perigee passes per period, which show up as p lobes in the rotating frame. The 2:1 is a figure-eight, the 3:1 a three-leaf clover, the 4:1 four petals and the 5:2 a five-petal flower that needs two months to close.
  • The Moon's repeated, evenly spaced tugs either cancel out, giving a very stable orbit (stability index ν = 1), or add up, giving an unstable one. Orbits that pass close to the Moon, such as the 3:2 here, are strongly unstable.
  • Real missions use them. NASA's TESS space telescope (2018) flies a 2:1 lunar-resonant orbit with its apogees about 90° away from the Moon, so it can stay in orbit for decades with almost no fuel. IBEX was moved into a 3:1 resonant orbit in 2011 for the same reason.

How the toolkit finds them

  1. Keplerian guess (resonant_guess): an Earth-only ellipse whose period is exactly q/p of the Moon's, starting at perigee on the far side of the Earth, where the velocity is perpendicular to the x-axis.
  2. Fixed-time correction (correct_symmetric_period): these orbits cross the x-axis several times per lap, so the usual "stop at the first crossing" corrector (section 6) does not work. Instead the toolkit flies exactly half a period and adjusts the perigee speed and the period together until the orbit crosses the x-axis at right angles there. A symmetric orbit that does that is periodic.
  3. Mass homotopy (automatic fallback): over two or three months the Moon can drag the guess too far for the corrector to recover. The toolkit then starts with an almost massless Moon, where the guess is exactly periodic, and raises the Moon's mass to its true value in small steps, re-correcting each time. The 5:2 orbit with e=0.5 needs this.

Not every combination exists. As the Moon's mass grows, some resonant orbits merge with other families and disappear. Then resonant raises an error saying so (for example 4:3 and 5:3 at their default eccentricity); try a different e.

from cislunar import periodic as P, frames
from cislunar.viz import plot_trajectories_2d

tess = P.resonant(2, 1, e=0.55)          # TESS-like orbit
print(tess.summary())                    # period ~27.2 d (two laps of 13.6 d), stability index 1.0
tr = tess.trajectory(3000)
plot_trajectories_2d(tr, "xy", origin="earth", fit="all")                    # the rosette
plot_trajectories_2d(frames.trajectory_to_inertial(tr, "earth"), "xy")        # the ellipse

flower = P.resonant(5, 2, e=0.5)         # needs the mass homotopy (~20 s)

10.2 Lunar rosettes

Now turn the idea around. The NRHO and the DRO are single closed loops in the rotating frame (section 6). But the rotating frame turns once per 27.3 days, so to someone watching from the stars, each loop is drawn a little further round than the last:

  • The 9:2 NRHO takes 6.56 days per loop, so each loop is rotated about 86° from the previous one. The petals all meet at the perilune over the Moon's north pole and fan out at apolune, 70,000 km south.
  • The 70,000 km DRO takes 13.9 days per loop, about half a month, so successive loops are turned by roughly 180° and the pattern becomes a slowly turning, rounded square.

Why it matters: an inertial rosette sets where the Sun is relative to the orbit on each pass, which drives lighting, eclipses and thermal conditions. It also sets which way the spacecraft's antennas and solar arrays point. The "9:2" in the NRHO's name is chosen so that this pattern stays in step with the Sun, repeating every two synodic months. The toolkit's CR3BP has no Sun, so here the rosette keeps turning slowly instead of closing exactly.

11. Advanced: transient orbits, flybys and Δv budgets

Most of this manual is about orbits that repeat. This section is about the ones that don't, or don't for long: unstable orbits, the "highways" that lead away from them, trajectories that are captured by the Moon for a while and then leave. It then covers how to turn all of that into a Δv budget: the number every mission is designed around.

KSP instinct check In KSP an orbit is either stable forever or you are on an escape or impact trajectory. In the three-body world there is a large middle ground: orbits that last for weeks or months and then leave, and orbits that only stay because someone nudges them every few days. "Unstable" is not a yes/no label; it is a time scale.

11.1 How fast errors grow

Every periodic orbit has a monodromy matrix: the table of how a small error changes over one lap. Its largest eigenvalue λ is the factor by which the worst-case error grows per lap, so the e-folding time T / ln λ is how long an error takes to grow by a factor of e (about 2.7). This is the planning number. It tells you how often you must track and correct the spacecraft.

Figure 12. Error-growth time across orbit families
E-folding time for every member of the families from Figure 4, computed from each orbit's stability index. DROs are stable (no growth), so they do not appear. Python: manifolds.efolding_time(orbit).

Small L1 and L2 halos double their errors in about a day; the NRHO takes about a week. A 1 m navigation error on a small L1 halo becomes 1 km in about 11 days; on the NRHO, in about 58.

11.2 Invariant manifolds: the free highways

The direction in which errors grow fastest is also a free ride. Nudge a spacecraft a tiny distance along it and it leaves the orbit on its own, with no further burns, following one of a family of trajectories called the unstable manifold. Run time backwards and the same idea gives the stable manifold: trajectories that drift onto the orbit without an arrival burn. Together they form tubes that pass through the L1 and L2 gates of section 5, and they are how low-energy transfers such as CAPSTONE's ballistic lunar transfer get by on so little fuel.

  • Interior branch: the first step goes toward the Earth; exterior: away from it.
  • A 50 km nudge along the unstable direction is equivalent to a burn of well under 1 m/s, yet carries the spacecraft out of the Moon's neighbourhood within a few weeks.
  • Stable orbits (the DRO) have no manifolds. manifold() raises an error for them.

from cislunar import manifolds as M, periodic as P

11.3 Where do trajectories go? Fates, lifetimes and FTLE

For a trajectory that is not periodic, the useful questions are what happens to it and when. The toolkit gives every trajectory near the Moon a fate:

  • leaves via L1: passes a plane 40,000 km beyond L1, heading toward Earth;
  • leaves via L2: passes a plane 40,000 km beyond L2, heading outward;
  • impacts the Moon (or Earth);
  • still captured: none of these within the time horizon.

The time of that event is the trajectory's lifetime. A second measure, the finite-time Lyapunov exponent (FTLE), says how fast two neighbouring trajectories separate. High FTLE marks the knife-edges between outcomes, where a tiny change in starting state flips the result. These ridges trace the manifold tubes from 11.2.

Each cell is one trajectory starting on the Earth–Moon line at the given distance from the Moon, moving perpendicular to it (retrograde or prograde around the Moon) with the speed set by the energy (Jacobi constant, as in Figure 3). 6,300 trajectories, each flown for 60 days. Click a cell to fly that trajectory in your browser (right). Python: manifolds.fate_map(d_km, C, "retrograde", horizon_days=60, with_ftle=True).

Reading the map: at high C (low energy, top) the L1 and L2 gates are closed. A spacecraft can only stay or fall into the Moon. Below C ≈ 3.19 the L1 gate opens, then below about 3.17 the L2 gate, and trajectories starting near the Moon begin to leave. Note how many starts are still captured after 60 days even though they are not on any periodic orbit. These are the temporary captures, the cislunar version of a "minimoon". Retrograde starts survive much better than prograde ones, for the same reason DROs are stable.

11.4 Flybys: paying only half

A rendezvous pays twice: once to leave, once to match the target's velocity. A flyby (for inspection or imaging) skips the second burn, so the spacecraft passes the target at the relative speed at encounter. That speed is a budget line in its own right: at 1 km/s you get a few seconds of close-range imaging and a high-consequence collision risk. Real inspection flybys also aim for a deliberate miss distance. targeting.flyby(..., miss_km=25) aims at a point offset perpendicular to the relative velocity, so that point is the true closest approach, and verifies the result with a dense search.

11.5 Statistical Δv: trajectory correction maneuvers

No burn is perfect. A departure burn that is slightly off grows into a large miss: the same error growth as in 11.1, now along a transfer. A trajectory correction maneuver (TCM) fixes it, and its cost depends on how early you make it. Because the cost is (to first order) proportional to the error, the toolkit computes it from the transfer's sensitivity matrix (the state transition matrix) by sampling thousands of possible errors. It then reports percentiles: budgets usually carry the 99th percentile ("p99"), the cost that 99% of real flights stay under. monte_carlo_tcm re-flies the transfer with full nonlinear dynamics, navigation errors and imperfect TCM execution to check that the shortcut holds.

Figure 16. TCM cost versus when you correct
Left: cost of a single TCM at each time after departure (log scale). Linear in the error size, so the slider rescales the curves exactly. Right: 400 nonlinear Monte Carlo runs of a two-TCM strategy (day 0.5 and day 3) for the LEO→NRHO transfer at 1 m/s per axis, against the linear prediction.
from cislunar import navigation as N
P0 = N.injection_covariance(1.0)                         # 1 m/s per axis
# or N.burn_execution_covariance(tr.dv1, mag_frac=0.01, pointing_deg=0.5)
lin = N.linear_tcm(x_post, tof, P0, S.nd_time_days([0.25, 0.5, 1, 2]), dv_arrival=dv2)
print(lin.table())
mc = N.monte_carlo_tcm(x_post, tof, P0, S.nd_time_days([0.5, 3.0]), target_state=x_target, n=400)
print(mc.summary())

11.6 Station-keeping: the price of staying

Staying on an unstable orbit means watching the error grow and nudging it back. The toolkit's controller uses Floquet modes: it decomposes the deviation from the orbit into its natural directions and cancels only the one that grows, with the smallest possible burn. Stable directions are left alone because they don't grow, so a stable orbit like the DRO needs nothing. Two subtleties show up in the results:

  • Tracking too often wastes fuel: each pass reacts to navigation noise. Tracking too rarely lets the error grow large, or, on fast-diverging orbits, loses the orbit altogether. Each orbit has a sweet spot, roughly 0.5–2 e-folding times.
  • Not every point on the orbit is a good place to burn. Near the NRHO's perilune a burn barely affects the growing direction, so the controller skips those passes and waits for a better one, as real NRHO operations do.
Figure 17. Station-keeping cost versus tracking interval
Floquet-mode Monte Carlo (12 runs of 180 days each, navigation 1 km and 1 cm/s, 2% execution error). Bars span mean to 95th percentile. × would mark intervals where control was lost. None are lost in this sweep, but stretch the interval far enough and it happens: stationkeeping_mc(P.halo(1, 15000, "north"), 20) loses the small L1 halo every time. Right: deviation from the orbit's path in one run, with burns marked. The model has no Sun or other unmodelled forces, so treat absolute values as lower bounds; the trends are what matter.

States and time

A state is six numbers [x, y, z, vx, vy, vz]: position and velocity in the rotating frame, nondimensional, measured from the barycentre. Time is nondimensional too; t = 0 is an arbitrary epoch. Functions that need "where is the target at time t" accept a PeriodicOrbit, an initial state (6,) or any function t → state.

16. Limitations

  • Idealised dynamics. The CR3BP has no Sun, a perfectly circular lunar orbit and point-mass bodies. Real NRHOs need a few cm/s to m/s per year of station-keeping that this model does not show, and transfer costs can differ by tens of m/s from high-fidelity tools.
  • Impulsive burns. Burns are instantaneous velocity changes. Long engine burns (low thrust, or big burns with small engines) are not modelled.
  • Single shooting. Very long or chaotic transfers may not converge; multiple shooting is planned.
  • LVLH definition. The LVLH frame is built from the target's velocity in the rotating frame (see architecture.md, ADR-4). Other tools may define it from the inertial velocity; the difference is small near perilune but noticeable near apolune.
  • Hold points. Waypoints are pass-through points with a burn, not station-keeping holds.
