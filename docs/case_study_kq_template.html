Who launched 2010 KQ? Tracing an unidentified rocket stage back to its launch

In May 2010 a survey telescope found a faint object a few metres across, circling the Sun on an orbit almost identical to the Earth's. Sunlight pushed it around too easily for a rock, and its colours matched weathered white paint. The Minor Planet Center deleted it from the asteroid catalogue as artificial. Sixteen years later nobody has said for certain which launch it came from. This case study takes the public 2010 observations, rebuilds the orbit with the cislunar toolkit, runs hundreds of possible orbits back to the start of the space age, and puts each published suspect on trial.

Headline results

1. The mystery

2010 KQ was found by the Catalina Sky Survey on 2010 May 16, five days before it passed 490,000 km from the Earth. Its orbit around the Sun had a period of 1.04 years, an eccentricity below 0.01 and an inclination of 0.07°, which is suspicious: a natural object on such an orbit would collide with the Earth within a few thousand years. JPL's Paul Chodas and Project Pluto's Bill Gray traced it back to a close pass by the Earth in the spring of 1975. Infrared spectra by S. J. Bus and filter colours by Richard Miles did not match any asteroid type; Miles found they matched white titanium-dioxide paint aged by sunlight, as measured on NASA's Long Duration Exposure Facility. In early July 2010 Miles's own astrometry showed that purely gravitational orbits no longer fitted, and the Minor Planet Center deleted the "asteroid" (MPEC 2010-N23).

Two candidate launches were then tried and discarded, and three remained:

Miles concluded (2011) that it is "highly likely" the final stage of a Soviet Moon probe, from Luna 1, Luna 4 or Luna 23, and could not narrow it further. Wikipedia's Luna 23 article now says it is "believed to be" that mission's rocket. This study asks whether the orbit itself can say which.

Sources: Project Pluto pseudo-MPEC for 2010 KQ = RK252A5 (Gray, 2010, with the astrometry); R. Miles, J. Br. Astron. Assoc. 121, 350 (2011); launch facts from Wikipedia (Luna 1, 4, 22, 23; Blok D). Everything outside sections 3–11 is from published reports, not from this analysis.

4. A model good for 50 years

Running an orbit back 35 years is a different job from fitting 5 weeks of data: the planets matter, and so does a subtle effect of relativity. Before trusting any of it, the same code was run backwards from 2010 for two natural asteroids whose orbits JPL knows well from decades of data, and compared with JPL's positions.

Figure 3. Error of the backward run against JPL, without and with relativity
3753 Cruithne and 1991 VG, run back from 2010 June 1 with all eight planets. Log scale. 1991 VG passed 460,000 km from the Earth in December 1991, which amplifies earlier errors, so its curve stops there.

6. Suspect: Luna 1 (1959)

Figure 5. A quantity that close encounters cannot change
The Tisserand parameter of 2010 KQ's orbit relative to the Earth, year by year, back to 1958 (nominal orbit). The line is Luna 1's stage. A pass by the Earth leaves this number almost unchanged; a value of exactly 3 means "barely moving relative to the Earth".
