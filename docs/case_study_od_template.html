A rocket stage, 111 telescope sightings, and a crater on the far side of the Moon

In January 2022 amateur and professional astronomers tracked a faint object looping around the Earth. Orbit calculations showed it would hit the Moon on 4 March 2022. It was first thought to be a SpaceX stage, then identified as a Chinese rocket stage launched in 2014. Three months after the impact, NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter photographed the new crater. This case study takes the 111 published sightings and runs them through the cislunar toolkit to see how far they get us, using a real crater as the answer key.

Headline results
How to read this Everything here uses only the observations from 15 to 20 January 2022, six weeks before impact. The professional predictions quoted for comparison also used February observations, which are not public in the source used here. The crater location comes from LRO and is used only to check the forecast, never in the fit.

1. The object

The object was posted as WE0913A in 2015 by the Catalina Sky Survey and then mostly ignored. In January 2022, Bill Gray (Project Pluto, author of the Find_Orb orbit software) noticed that its orbit would take it into the Moon on 4 March. He first identified it as the second stage of the SpaceX Falcon 9 that launched NASA's DSCOVR spacecraft in February 2015. On 12 February 2022, Jon Giorgini of JPL pointed out that DSCOVR's trajectory didn't pass close enough to the Moon to fit. Gray then traced the object back to the Long March 3C third stage that launched China's Chang'e 5-T1 lunar test mission on 23 October 2014. That stage passed the Moon five days after launch. Other evidence pointed the same way: an amateur-radio cubesat launched with it has a matching orbit, and University of Arizona spectra resemble Chinese boosters rather than Falcon 9s. China's foreign ministry denied it was the Chang'e 5 stage, which Gray attributes to a mix-up between the Chang'e 5 and 5-T1 missions.

The stage hit the Moon at about 12:25 UTC on 4 March 2022 on the far side, near the crater Hertzsprung, out of sight from Earth. On 21 May 2022, LRO imaged an unusual double crater there, roughly 28 m across, at 5.226°N, 234.486°E. A double crater suggests an object with heavy masses at both ends, which fits a spent rocket stage.

Sources: Project Pluto pseudo-MPEC (astrometry, Find_Orb orbit, impact prediction); identification correction; crater found; LROC: "Mystery Rocket Body Found!" (M. Robinson, 23 June 2022); MPC observatory codes; Moon and Sun positions from JPL Horizons.

2. The observations

Each observation is a time and a direction: the object's right ascension and declination, measured against catalogue stars in a CCD image. There is no distance and no velocity. Nine observatories in Europe and the US contributed. Two French observatories, SATINO (C95) and PASTIS (K19), took most of them, in rapid series of 3 to 10 frames.

3. Modelling a telescope measurement

To fit an orbit we must predict, for any trial orbit, exactly what each telescope would have measured. Getting this to a fraction of an arcsecond (1″ is the width of a coin seen from 5 km) takes several corrections that are easy to miss:

  • Where the telescope is. The observatory's position on the spinning Earth, rotated into the star-catalogue frame (J2000) using Earth rotation, precession (0.3° of drift since 2000) and nutation.
  • Light travel time. The light left the object about 1 second before it was recorded. In that time the object moved about a kilometre.
  • The Earth's motion. Star-catalogue positions are measured from the solar system's centre of mass. While the light is in transit, the Earth moves about 38 km along its orbit around the Sun. Leaving this out caused a 20″ error in the first version of this model.

5. Why the Sun matters

The rest of this toolkit uses the circular restricted three-body problem (CR3BP): Earth and Moon only, with the Moon on a circular orbit. That model is excellent for designing orbits but not for fitting real measurements of this object. At 300,000–500,000 km from Earth, the Sun's tidal pull moves the object by thousands of kilometres within a week. The same 111 observations fitted with each force model:

Figure 3. How well each force model can fit the real observations

8. Sunlight pressure

Sunlight pushes on everything it hits. For a 12-metre empty rocket stage the push is tiny, about a ten-billionth of Earth's surface gravity. Over six weeks it adds up to hundreds of kilometres. Its strength depends on the stage's area-to-mass ratio A/m and how reflective it is (Cr). These enter only as the product CrA/m, which nobody knows in advance. 9. Through three-body eyes

In the CR3BP, the Jacobi constant C plays the role of energy. It decides which regions an object can reach: below the value at the L1 and L2 points (about 3.19 and 3.17), the zero-velocity curves open and the Moon's neighbourhood becomes reachable. Figure 9. The "constant" that isn't
Jacobi value along the forecast trajectory. In the CR3BP it would be a flat line. The Sun and the Moon's eccentric orbit make it drift, mostly near perigee passes. The object stays well below the L1/L2 values, so the CR3BP's energy barrier never closes, but whether it actually hits the Moon depends on details the CR3BP doesn't have.

10. What this does and doesn't show

  • The orbit fit is solid. It reproduces the observations to their noise level, and its elements agree with Find_Orb's to within the stated uncertainties.
  • The forecast is only as good as the force model. With gravity alone, the January data put the impact within about the right distance along the track, but off to one side of the crater. Allowing a modest sunlight pressure puts the track through the crater. With only January data, though, that pressure is fitted to the answer; it isn't measured. February data would have measured it.
  • The fitted pressure is lower than a simple estimate. An empty stage 12.4 m × 3 m with about 2.8 t dry mass, tumbling, would have CrA/m ≈ 0.01–0.017 m²/kg. The value that hits the crater is lower. It may be absorbing other model errors: Earth's shadow, a non-spherical stage, residual propellant, or the Moon's gravity field. Treat it as an effective parameter, not a measurement.
  • Not modelled: Earth-shadow passages, relativity, planets other than the Sun, lunar gravity harmonics on the final approach, and per-site timing biases.

11. Reproduce it

from cislunar import astrometry as A, ephemeris as E, od
from cislunar.constants import EARTH_MOON as S
recs = A.parse("docs/data/change5t1/astrometry.txt")          # 111 observations
st = A.load_stations("docs/data/change5t1/stations.txt")      # MPC parallax constants
eph = E.Ephemeris.cached("docs/data/change5t1/ephemeris_2022.json", "2022-01-10", "2022-03-06")
dyn = E.NBodyDynamics(eph, E.utc_to_tdb(recs[0].jd_utc))       # Earth+Moon+Sun (+J2), J2000
obs = A.to_observations(recs, st, dyn)                          # light-time + barycentric aberration
jd, r, v = A.gauss_iod([recs[40], recs[60], recs[80]], st)[0]   # first orbit from three directions
x = dyn.propagate_many(S.state_to_nd(list(r) + list(v)), float(dyn.t_of_jd(E.utc_to_tdb(jd))), [0.0], False)[0][0]
fit = od.fit(obs, x, 0.0, S, dynamics=dyn)                      # batch least squares
t, X, impact = dyn.propagate(fit.x, 0.0, float(dyn.t_of_jd(2459644.0)), moon_radius_km=1739.26)
print(impact["lat"], impact["lon"], E.tdb_to_utc(impact["jd_tdb"]))

Full pipeline: python docs/build_case_study_od.py --rebuild (about 10 minutes on 7 cores). The data files, with their sources, are in docs/data/change5t1/.
