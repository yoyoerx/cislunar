Rock or rocket? Two objects the Earth caught, and how to tell them apart

Every so often the Earth briefly captures a small object, which loops around us for a few weeks or months and then leaves. Some are natural boulders. Some are old rocket stages coming home. From a telescope both look like the same faint moving dot. This case study takes two of them, 2020 SO and 2024 PT5, runs the published observations through exactly the same cislunar pipeline, and lets the orbits say which is which.

Headline results

1. Two visitors

2020 SO was found by the Pan-STARRS survey in September 2020 and catalogued as a small asteroid. Its orbit around the Sun was almost identical to the Earth's, with low inclination and very low speed relative to us, which is suspicious for a natural object. It was captured by the Earth from November 2020 to March 2021. Paul Chodas at JPL traced its orbit back to a close pass by the Earth in September 1966, matching the launch of NASA's Surveyor 2 lunar lander. Spectra from NASA's Infrared Telescope Facility later matched painted stainless steel. It is the Centaur upper stage that sent Surveyor 2 toward the Moon. It has since been removed from the asteroid catalogues, so today the Minor Planet Center and JPL's small-body database both answer "not found".

2024 PT5 was found by the ATLAS survey in August 2024. It is about 10 m across and was temporarily captured from late September to late November 2024; news reports called it the Earth's "second moon". Its spectrum resembles lunar rock, which suggests it may be a fragment blasted off the Moon by an old impact. Nobody has suggested it is artificial.

Sources: Project Pluto pseudo-MPEC for 2020 SO = Surveyor 2 Centaur (astrometry and Find_Orb orbit); 2024 PT5 astrometry from the Minor Planet Center and JPL's orbit from Horizons / SBDB; Moon and Sun from JPL Horizons. The background on each object's identification is from published reports, not from this analysis.

2. The idea: sunlight as a scale

Sunlight carries momentum, so it pushes on whatever it hits. The push is proportional to the area facing the Sun, and the acceleration it causes is inversely proportional to mass. So what matters is the area-to-mass ratio A/m, multiplied by a reflectivity factor Cr between about 1 and 2.

  • A solid rock 5–15 m across, with density 2–3.5 g/cm³, has CrA/m of roughly 0.00003–0.0002 m²/kg.
  • An empty rocket stage is a thin-walled tank. A Centaur, about 9 m long, 3 m wide and 2 t empty, tumbling, has CrA/m of roughly 0.01–0.02 m²/kg.

That is a factor of about a hundred. You can't weigh an object 1,000,000 km away, but its orbit records how hard sunlight has been pushing on it. Over weeks to months the push displaces a hollow object by thousands of kilometres and a rock by tens. The test: fit each orbit with CrA/m as a free parameter and see where it lands.

7. Were they really captured?

"Captured" usually means that, measured from the Earth, the object's orbital energy is negative (it is bound to the Earth, not just passing), while it is within a few Hill radii. The Hill radius, 1.5 million km, is where the Earth's pull and the Sun's tidal pull balance. This is a three-body problem, but the relevant pair is the Sun and the Earth, not the Earth and the Moon. The toolkit's CR3BP machinery works the same way for any pair of bodies; the Sun–Earth L1 and L2 points sit about 1.5 million km sunward and anti-sunward of the Earth.

Figure 6. Distance from the Earth through each capture
Time is measured from each object's closest approach. The dashed line is the Hill radius. Thick segments are where the object was bound to the Earth (negative geocentric energy, within three Hill radii).
Figure 7. The captures seen from a frame that turns with the Earth around the Sun
Earth at the centre, Sun far to the left. The circle is the Hill sphere and the crosses are the Sun–Earth L1 and L2 points. 2020 SO comes in near L1, loops around the Earth inside the Hill sphere, and leaves near L2. 2024 PT5 swept through the Hill sphere in July–August, too fast to be held, then drifted outward. Its energy-defined "capture" happened later, 3.5–4 million km out (lower right).
Figure 8. The Sun–Earth Jacobi value
In the Sun–Earth three-body problem the Jacobi constant sets which regions are reachable. Just below the L1/L2 values (dashed) only a narrow neck around L1 and L2 is open, which is the regime of slow, looping temporary captures like 2020 SO's. Far below, as for 2024 PT5, the region around the Earth is wide open and an object can wander past freely.

9. Limits

  • Sunlight pressure is modelled as a steady push away from the Sun (a "cannonball"). A tumbling stage presents a changing area, and reflected and re-emitted heat add small sideways pushes. The fitted value is an average effective number.
  • Planets other than the Earth are left out. Over a few months near the Earth their effect is small, but not zero. For 2024 PT5, which spent months millions of km from the Earth, their pull could account for part of its small measured push.
  • For 2020 SO only the November–January part of the published arc is used here (Project Pluto lists earlier observations back to August 2020).
  • The expected ranges for rocks and stages in section 2 are rough. The point is the factor of about a hundred between them, not the exact edges.
  • The identification of 2020 SO as Surveyor 2's Centaur rests on JPL's back-integration to 1966 and on spectra. This analysis independently confirms the hollow-object signature, not the specific launch.
