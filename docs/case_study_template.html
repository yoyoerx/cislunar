Where should a satellite-servicing base station live?

A case study using the cislunar toolkit. It compares eight candidate base stations, from a LEO depot to stations near the Moon, as homes for reusable servicing vehicles. Their clients are satellites in nine orbit families: LEO, MEO, GEO, translunar, cislunar and lunar. The study covers the Δv to field each station, the Δv of every servicing route, and what 10, 100 or 1,000 servicing missions add up to.

Headline results (default assumptions; they update as you change the model below)
How to read the numbers Δv figures are computed by the toolkit's three-body model and are reliable to a few percent for the routes and orbits defined here. Campaign totals combine those Δv values with a mass model: vehicle masses, engine efficiency and lifetimes. That model is an assumption, so every one of those inputs is a slider in section 6. Treat the campaign results as a structured comparison, not a cost estimate.

What the numbers say

Figures in this section use the default mass model (20 t station, 2 t reusable servicer, 500 kg kit per mission, Isp 320 s, servicer replaced every 20 missions, 10-year station life). The interactive sections recompute everything as you change it.

  1. Plane changes decide the Earth-side market. LEO and GNSS clients sit in steeply inclined planes. A launcher can put a servicer straight into the client's plane, so flying direct costs only 164 m/s (SSO) or 3.4 km/s (GNSS). Any single base station must instead pay the plane change: 8.6 km/s from a 28.5° LEO depot to SSO, and 3.8–4.2 km/s from every high station. No station in this study ever beats direct launches for LEO or MEO clients.
  2. Cislunar space is a tight network. Any two of the NRHO, DRO, L1 and L2 halos and the 2:1 translunar orbit are 0.17–0.69 km/s apart (L1 ↔ L2 halo is the cheapest, at 174 m/s). Low lunar orbit is 0.68–0.95 km/s from the libration orbits. Reaching any of them from LEO costs 3.6–4.4 km/s. Once something is in cislunar space, moving it around is cheap.
  3. GEO is the hinge. From high orbits GEO is 1.4–2.1 km/s away (cheapest from the 2:1 translunar orbit at 1,364 m/s and the L1 halo at 1,417 m/s), against 4.2 km/s from LEO. Plane changes are cheap far from Earth. But a reusable servicer pays that twice (there and back, with the kit aboard), and its propellant must first be shipped up to the station. So with the default masses, serving GEO from a remote station costs 28–34% more than flying direct, while a depot in GEO itself saves 65% at 100 missions and 73% at 1,000.
  4. Ten missions never justify a station; about a dozen do. With a station in the client's own orbit, break-even comes after 12–15 missions for every GEO, translunar, cislunar and lunar class. A cislunar station serving neighbouring cislunar orbits breaks even after 12–31 missions (DRO serving the L2 halo: 14; L1 halo serving the L2 halo: 12). Serving LLO from a libration-orbit station takes 20–90.
  5. For a mixed customer base, two stations beat one. In the default mix (35% GEO, 25% SSO, 10% GNSS, 30% translunar, cislunar and lunar) and 100 missions, the best plan is a GEO depot plus one cislunar station, with LEO and GNSS clients served by direct launches. It needs about 540 t in LEO against 766 t with no station, a saving of about 30%. The cislunar partner barely matters: DRO, L1 halo and NRHO are within about 3% of each other. So choose it on other grounds: the DRO needs no station-keeping, the L1 halo is closest to GEO and LLO, and the NRHO shares Gateway's infrastructure.
  6. What would change the answer. A lighter servicer relative to its kit, a higher-Isp engine, or longer servicer life all favour remote stations. So does lunar propellant, if it can be delivered for less than about half the cost per kg of propellant launched to LEO (try the slider in section 6). The rest of this report lets you test each of these.

2. The question

A servicing mission sends a robotic vehicle (the servicer) to a client satellite to refuel it, repair it, upgrade it or attach a life-extension pod. It leaves behind a servicing kit of propellant, parts or a pod. There are two ways to run a servicing business:

ArchitectureHow each mission fliesKSP analogy
DirectLaunch a new servicer and kit to LEO, fly LEO → client, and stay attached to the client (the servicer is used once)Launch a fresh rescue ship for every stranded Kerbal
Base stationField a station once. A reusable servicer lives there and flies station → client → station each mission. Kits and propellant are shipped up from LEOBuild a refuelling station in orbit and run a reusable tug from it

A station costs a lot up front but makes each later mission cheaper, if it sits somewhere that is cheap to reach the clients from. High orbits have a hidden advantage: changing the orbital plane is cheap far from Earth, where you move slowly, and very expensive in LEO. A station near the Moon can reach GEO, or an inclined orbit, without paying LEO's plane-change bill.

Every route here is computed, not looked up: the toolkit searches for the minimum-Δv two-burn transfer between each pair of orbits, using the full Earth–Moon three-body model (servicing.node_transfer).

4. Route costs: what each trip costs

Each cell is the minimum total Δv (departure burn + arrival burn) to fly from a station orbit to a rendezvous with a client in another orbit, assuming the servicer can wait for good timing. The diagonal (station and client sharing an orbit) is the cost of phasing: catching up with a client a quarter of an orbit ahead within about a month. Returns cost the same as the outbound trip (by a symmetry of the three-body problem, spot-checked below).

6. Campaign economics: 10, 100 or 1,000 missions

Δv alone cannot compare architectures: one km/s for a 20-tonne station is not the same as one km/s for a 2-tonne servicer. So campaigns are scored by the rocket equation, as mass needed in LEO (IMLEO). That includes everything that must be launched: the station, the servicers and their replacements, the kits, and all the propellant to move them, including the propellant to ship propellant up to the station.

9. Break-even: how many missions pay for a station?

The number of missions after which a station needs less total mass in LEO than flying every mission directly. "never" means the station's per-mission cost is not lower than going direct, however many missions are flown.

12. Reproduce it

from cislunar import servicing as SV
nodes = SV.default_nodes()                       # the 10 orbits above
r = SV.node_transfer(nodes["nrho"], nodes["geo"])  # one route (~1-2 min)
print(r.dv1_mps, r.dv2_mps, r.tof_days)
costs = SV.cost_matrix()                          # every route, parallel (~30 min); run under if __name__ == "__main__"
mm = SV.MassModel(station_kg=20000, servicer_kg=2000, kit_kg=500, isp_s=320)
for n in (10, 100, 1000):
    print(n, SV.campaign(n, "geo", "nrho", costs, mm)["imleo_kg"],
             SV.campaign(n, "geo", None, costs, mm)["imleo_kg"])
print(SV.break_even("geo", "nrho", costs, mm))
